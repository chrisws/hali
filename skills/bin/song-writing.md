# Song Writing

A skill for assisting the user with their own songwriting. You are a sparring
partner, not a ghostwriter. Suggest options, ask questions, offer alternatives.
Never just write the verse for them. Help them find their voice, don't replace it.

## Core Principle: Stable vs. Unstable

Everything in song structure serves one question: *how am I feeling?*

- **Stable** — a fact, resolved, grounded. "I'm so grateful you are in my life."
- **Unstable** — something missing, longing, unresolved. "I wish you were here."

Every structural choice (line count, line length, rhyme scheme, rhyme type,
rhythm) either creates stability or instability. Ask yourself what feeling the
section needs, then choose the tools that support it.

Stable ideas suit statements of fact, gratitude, resolution.
Unstable ideas suit longing, questions, tension, something missing.

## The Six Best Friends

When starting a new song, answer these six questions to ground the idea:

1. **Who** — who is the subject?
2. **What** — what is happening?
3. **When** — what time period?
4. **Where** — what setting?
5. **Why** — what's the motivation?
6. **How** — how does it unfold?

These aren't just background detail. They generate the specific, vivid images
that make a lyric feel real rather than generic.

## Point of View

Choose early — it shapes everything:

- **1st person narrative** — "I, she" (the singer tells their own story)
- **2nd person narrative** — "you, she" (the singer addresses someone)
- **3rd person narrative** — "he, she" (the singer observes a story)
- **Direct address** — "I, you" (the singer speaks directly to the listener)

Ask: Who is talking? To whom? Why? The answer determines the emotional
distance between singer and subject.

## The Three Boxes (Development Engine)

A song develops through three stages of a story:

- **Box 1 (Beginning)** — Introduce the situation. Set the scene.
- **Box 2 (Middle)** — Develop the conflict. Complicate. Ask questions.
- **Box 3 (End)** — Resolve. Change. The character is different than they
  started.

The story should *gain weight* as it develops. Box 1 is light and open.
Box 2 adds tension and complexity. Box 3 delivers the emotional payoff.

Example pattern:
- Box 1: "Hi, what's up?" (introduction, open)
- Box 2: "Where have you been? Where are you going?" (development, questions)
- Box 3: "I wish I could go with you." (resolution, longing)

The boxes don't have to be chronological. They can be past/present/future,
or external/internal. The key is that each box *adds* something the previous
box didn't have.

## Five Compositional Elements

These are the tools for creating stability or instability. Use them together.

### 1. Number of Lines

- **Even** number of lines → stable, complete, resolved
- **Odd** number of lines → unstable, unbalanced, moves forward

An odd number creates forward motion and momentum. It doesn't resolve, so the
listener is pushed into the next section.

Effects:
- Prosody (support the emotion)
- Spotlighting important ideas
- Stopping motion (even) or moving one section into another (odd)
- Creating contrast between sections (e.g., a 4-line verse into a 5-line chorus)

### 2. Length of Lines

- **Matched** line lengths → stable, complete, resolved (a fact)
- **Unmatched** line lengths → unstable

Specific patterns:
- Second line **shorter** than first → unstable, prompts for more, longing
- Second line **longer** than first → spotlights the final text, emphasis

Effects:
- Prosody
- Spotlight important ideas with a longer line
- Move one section to another (stable = satisfied, unstable = forward)
- Contrast sections

### 3. Rhyme Scheme

The pattern of rhymed (a, b, c...) and unrhymed (x) end lines.

- **a/a** (couplet) — most stable, two equal lines with end-line rhyme
- **a/b/a/b** — stable, avalanche of power, second line gets spotlight
- **a/a/b/b** — stable with a pause, two equal ideas
- **a/b/b/a** (envelope) — unstable, more to say, uncertain
- **x/x** — no rhyme, deceptive cadence, most unstable

Grouping by rhyme scheme creates different feelings:
- **abab** — stable, powerful
- **aabb** — stable with a pause
- **abba** — unstable, eulogistic, "more to say"
- **x/x** — open, unresolved

### 4. Rhyme Types

From most resolved (stable) to least resolved (unstable):

#### Perfect Rhyme
Most resolved. Identical vowel sounds AND identical following consonants.
- mud / blood
- trees / breeze

A sonic event: tension → resolution. Difference → similarity.
Note: repetition of the same word is *identity*, not rhyme.

English is a rhyme-poor language (only ~17 vowel sounds), so perfect rhymes
are relatively rare and carry strong resolution.

#### Family Rhyme
Same vowel sound, but the following consonants are from the same *phonetic
family* (not identical).

Consonant families:
- **Plosives (voiced):** b, d, g
- **Plosives (unvoiced):** p, t, k
- **Fricatives (voiced):** v, th, z, zh, j
- **Fricatives (unvoiced):** f, th, s, sh, ch
- **Nasals (voiced):** m, n, ng

Example: "my heart's been stuck in the mud" — the /d/ can rhyme with /t/,
/k/, /g/ sounds (same plosive family).

Finding family rhymes: identify the vowel sound, then look for words ending
in consonants from the same family.
- mud → /d/ → plosive family → stuck, plug, tug, ...
- mud → /ʌ/ vowel → /ʌk/ → /ʌg/ → /ʌt/

#### Additive Rhyme
Two words where the second *adds* a sound not in the first. More stable.
- cry / ride (adds /d/, a voiced plosive — least addition, most stable)
- cry / smile (adds more — less stable)
- free / speed
- glow / stove
- fit / grits
- fine / resigned

The less sound you add, the more stable. A voiced plosive is the smallest
possible addition.

#### Subtractive Rhyme
The reverse of additive — something is *missing*. Unstable.
- speed / free (the /d/ is missing)
- stove / glow
- reigned / fine
- smile / cry

Creates a feeling of something absent, incomplete.

#### Assonance Rhyme
Only the vowel sound is shared. Ending consonants are from *different*
phonetic families. Always unstable.
- life / tide (ii/ii, but F/D — different families)
- blood / rush (uu/uu)
- fool / rude (oo/oo)
- fire / smile (ii/ii)

Use when talking about something diminished, unstable, or when you want a
feeling of connection without resolution. Diminishes acceleration.

#### Consonance Rhyme
The ending consonants are identical, but the vowel sounds are *different*.
Least resolved. Possibly not even a rhyme — just a sound relationship.
- friend / wind (different vowels, same /nd/)
- one / alone
- scars / fears
- filled / crawled
- thread / road
- fire / tower

Opens the gate to more emotion. Very expressive. A song is a vowel-exaggerated
medium, so consonance keeps the vowel open and expressive.

#### Summary (most stable → least stable)

| Type | Example | Feeling |
|------|---------|---------|
| Perfect | fun / sun | Resolved, complete |
| Family | fun / rum | Warm, connected |
| Additive | fun / lunch | Slight addition, mostly stable |
| Subtractive | lunch / fun | Something missing |
| Assonance | fun / love | Connection, no resolution |
| Consonance | fun / on | Open, expressive, unresolved |

### 5. Rhythm of Lines

How the phrase starts relative to the downbeat of the bar.

**Front-heavy** (starts on the downbeat):
- Weight is in the front of the line
- Starts in the strongest, brightest position
- Sounds grounded, "I mean it"
- Stable — attached, then spreads over the bar

**Back-heavy** (starts after the downbeat):
- In motion *toward* a strong beat
- Feels unstable, puzzling
- Sounds uncertain
- The phrasing creates body language

The same two lines can feel four different ways depending on where they
enter relative to the bar:

```
Strong bar:  1   2   3   4    Weak bar:  1   2   3   4
A:  Where are you going?      what did I do?
B:      Where are you going?  what did I do?
C:  Where are you going?      what did I do?
D:      Where are you going?         what did I do?
```

Each placement creates a different emotional combination.

## Rhyme as a Search Engine (The Worksheet)

This is a practical technique for generating ideas:

1. **Pick a keyword** from your song concept.
2. **Identify its vowel sound** and consonant ending.
3. **Find rhyme families** — list words that rhyme (perfect, family, additive,
   subtractive, assonance, consonance).
4. **Use the rhymes to generate ideas.** The rhyming words become a pool of
   sonically-compatible concepts to choose from.
5. **Select from the pool** based on meaning, not just sound.

Tips:
- Pay attention to the vowel sounds of your key words. It's better if they
  have *different* vowel sounds (gives more variety).
- Use a thesaurus to find neighboring ideas *before* going to a rhyming
  dictionary.
- If you already have a series of sonically-related ideas, you can choose
  from that set.
- Rhyme stressed syllables.
- For multi-syllable words, rhyme the *primary stress* syllable.

Example worksheet for the keyword "wind":
- Perfect: (none common — "wind" is a difficult rhyme)
- Family: hymn, whim, swim, sing, spring
- Assonance: drift
- Consonance: bend, friend
- (Note: "in" and "been" are not included — different vowel)

For "path":
- Perfect: (identify from list)
- Family: roar, door, soaring, shores
- Consonance: clear, shear, tear, air, dare, flair, prayer, share, stairs

For "wander" (rhyme the "wand" syllable):
- Dawn, gone, beyond, vagabond, marathon

## Melodic Rhythm and Setting

A line's length is determined by the number of *stressed* syllables.

### Stressed vs. Unstressed Syllables

**Stressed (content/meaning words):**
- Nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs
- Multi-syllable words: the primary stress gets the highest pitch
  - mul-(ti)-syl-(la)-ble → "syl" is the primary stress
  - Secondary stresses preserve the natural shape of the language

**Unstressed (grammatical/function words):**
- Articles: a, the, an
- Conjunctions: and, but, yet, if, or
- Prepositions: in, on, at, into, before, after
- Personal pronouns: I, you, he, she, it, they, us, them

These serve a grammatical function and are not stressed (unless presenting a
contrast). They are lower in pitch.

### Setting (Matching Lyric Rhythm to Melodic Rhythm)

In 4/4 time: strong on beat 1, weak on beat 2, strong on beat 3, weak on beat 4.

**Rule: Stressed syllables should land on strong positions in the bar.**

- Prepositions go *outside* spotlighted areas
- Preserve the natural shape of the language
- The primary stress of a multi-syllable word should align with a strong beat

Example: "(Hum)ming through the (walls)"
- "Hum" and "walls" are stressed → land on strong beats
- "ming" and "through" are unstressed → land on weak beats

## Body Language and Phrasing

Communication is 7% words, 35% tone of voice, the rest is body language.
In a song, *phrasing* is the body language.

Where a phrase enters relative to the downbeat changes the emotional meaning:

- **On the downbeat (front-heavy):** grounded, declarative, "I mean it"
- **After the downbeat (back-heavy):** in motion, uncertain, puzzling

A phrase that lands on the downbeat of a *weak* bar feels unstable even if
the words are stable. This creates contrast.

Example: A verse where the final line enters on the downbeat of a weak bar
feels unstable, setting up the next section as still unresolved.

## Stable and Unstable Tones (Harmony)

In any key, some scale degrees are stable, others are unstable:

**Stable:**
- Tonic (1 / do)
- Octave (8 / do)
- Perfect 5th (5 / sol)
- Major 3rd (3 / mi)

**Unstable (want to resolve):**
- 2nd (re) → wants to resolve to do
- 4th (fa) → wants to resolve to mi
- 6th (la) → wants to resolve to sol
- 7th (ti) → wants to resolve to do

Unstable tones are as expressive as the stable ones — they create the *wanting
to move, go somewhere* feeling. They don't have to resolve; they can be used
to colour ideas.

When a chord changes, previously stable notes can become unstable.
Example: In A minor, the notes A-C-E are stable. Move to G major, and those
same notes become unstable in the new context.

## Melody, Harmony, Rhythm

- **Melody** = the noun (creates the meaning)
- **Harmony** = the adjective (colours the meaning)
- **Rhythm** = the verb (the action)

The melody carries the core idea. The harmony colours it (sweet love vs.
disappointed love). The rhythm drives it forward.

Changing the harmony (the adjective) gives a different colour to the same
melodic idea (the noun) without changing the words.

## Workflow: New Song

When the user has a new song idea and needs to get started:

1. **Ground the idea** — Walk through the six best friends (who, what, when,
   where, why, how). Ask the user what they're feeling.
2. **Choose point of view** — 1st/2nd/3rd person, direct address. Who is
   talking, to whom, why?
3. **Find a metaphor** — A concrete image or object that carries the emotional
   weight. This gives you a keyword to work from.
4. **Outline the three boxes** — What goes in the beginning, middle, and end?
   How does the story gain weight?
5. **Make a worksheet** — Pick 2-4 keywords. Find their rhyme families. Build
   a pool of sonically-compatible ideas.
6. **Draft the structure** — Decide on number of lines, line lengths, rhyme
   scheme for each section. Check: does the stability/instability match the
   emotional content?
7. **Write provisional lines** — See how they move. What expectations do they
   raise? Do they say something?
8. **Refine** — Check each line against the five elements.

Tips for the process:
- Never build a bridge until you know what land masses it will join. Write
  the verses first, then the bridge as a contrasting device.
- Use statement/variation instead of statement/repetition where possible.
- Stimulate the listener's imagination. Start their own movie. Put their
  stuff in your song.
- A verse can use the title as its last line (verse/refrain form).
- Odd number of lines in a section pushes forward to the next.
- A shorter second line creates longing. A longer second line spotlights.

## Workflow: Partial Song

When the user has some sections (e.g., verse 1 and chorus) but is stuck on
others (e.g., verse 2):

1. **Identify what exists** — Read what they have. What's the emotional arc?
   What's stable, what's unstable?
2. **Identify what's missing** — What does the story need next? What would Box 2
   or Box 3 add that Box 1 didn't have?
3. **Check the structural balance** — If verse 1 is unstable and the chorus is
   stable, what should verse 2 be? Does it need to develop the instability
   further, or start resolving?
4. **Suggest rhyme options** — Based on the existing rhyme scheme, offer 3-5
   possible end words for each line. Let the user pick.
5. **Suggest line lengths** — Should the new section match or contrast the
   existing ones?
6. **Offer a draft, not a finished product** — "Here's a direction: [suggestion].
   Does that feel right, or should we try a different angle?"

## Workflow: Refining

When the user has a draft and wants to tighten it:

Walk through the five elements line by line:

1. **Number of lines** — Does the count support the feeling? Even = stable,
   odd = forward motion.
2. **Line lengths** — Are matched/unmatched lengths creating the right
   stability? Is there a spotlight where one is needed?
3. **Rhyme scheme** — Does the pattern (abab, aabb, abba, xx) match the
   emotional content?
4. **Rhyme types** — Is each rhyme the right *type* for the feeling? Perfect
   for resolution, consonance for openness, assonance for connection without
   closure?
5. **Rhythm** — Do stressed syllables land on strong beats? Are function words
   in weak positions? Is the phrasing front-heavy or back-heavy where intended?

Also check:
- Are the most important words spotlighted?
- Is the language specific and vivid (not generic)?
- Does each section *add* something the previous one didn't?
- Is the title interesting and does it appear where it has the most impact?
- Does the song gain weight from start to finish?

## Principles to Keep in Mind

- **Prosody** — all elements work together to support the central message.
  An appropriate relationship between elements.
- **Specific beats generic** — "trees lie burnt on the ground" beats "bad
  things happened."
- **Contrast creates interest** — stable verse into unstable chorus, or vice
  versa. Same energy throughout is flat.
- **The bridge is a contrasting device** — different note lengths, different
  rhyme scheme, different point of view. It should feel like a shift.
- **Unstable doesn't mean bad** — it means *in motion*. A song that's stable
  throughout has nowhere to go.
- **Rhyme is a search engine for ideas, not a constraint** — use it to find
  words you wouldn't have thought of, then choose based on meaning.
- **Preserve the natural shape of the language** — don't force a syllable
  into a beat if it makes the phrase sound unnatural.
- **A song is a vowel-exaggerated medium** — the vowels carry the emotion.
  Consonance rhymes keep vowels open and expressive.

## What NOT to Do

- Don't write the whole song for the user. Offer options, ask what feels right.
- Don't force a rhyme that doesn't serve the meaning.
- Don't make every section the same length and structure — variety creates
  interest.
- Don't resolve too early. Let tension build.
- Don't use the same rhyme type throughout. Mix them for different effects.
- Don't ignore the emotional content in favour of technical correctness.
  The feeling comes first; the structure serves it.
